# Research — How do dolphins sleep without drowning?

## Facts (with sources)

1. **Dolphins breathe voluntarily, not automatically.** Their breathing is consciously
   controlled, so they must stay conscious to keep breathing; going into a deep
   unconscious sleep would mean suffocation.
   Source: Whale and Dolphin Conservation (WDC), "How do dolphins sleep?" —
   https://au.whales.org/whales-dolphins/how-do-dolphins-sleep

2. **Unihemispheric slow-wave sleep:** dolphins rest only one brain hemisphere at a
   time while the other half stays alert. Source: WDC (same page); term widely used in
   the marine-mammal literature (e.g., Lyamin et al., 2008, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral
   Reviews*, "Sleep in unihemispheric animals").

3. **The awake half keeps them breathing and watching for danger.** Source: WDC (same page).

4. **One eye open:** the eye on the *opposite* side of the sleeping hemisphere stays open
   (each hemisphere mainly controls the opposite eye). When the right half of the brain
   sleeps, the left eye closes. Source: WDC (same page).

5. **How they rest:** dolphins rest motionless at the surface, or swim slowly and steadily
   near the surface; in shallow water they sometimes lie on the seabed and rise regularly
   to breathe. Source: WDC (same page).

6. **Sleep budget:** over a 24-hour period, each half of the brain gets roughly 4 hours of
   sleep. Source: Coral World Ocean Park (aquarium education); widely reported figure.

7. **Breath capacity:** a resting dolphin surfaces to breathe every few minutes; on average
   it can hold its breath around 7–10 minutes. Source: standard bottlenose-dolphin figure
   reported by marine parks/educators.

8. **Newborns don't rest:** for the first month after birth, bottlenose dolphin calves and
   their mothers show almost no normal sleep; both stay active and surface constantly.
   Source: Lyamin, Ponomareva & Mukhametov (study of captive bottlenose dolphins); popular
   summary: "Newborn dolphins and orcas don't sleep for the first month" (widely reported).

9. **Why this matters:** sleeping with one eye open lets the group keep watch for predators
   (sharks) while still resting. Source: WDC; Lyamin et al. 2008.
